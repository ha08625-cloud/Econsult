# Encoder training: does the fever head fail on the model or on the libraries?

*Write-up of the five-fold run on 2026-08-09. The numbers are in
`fever_present.arm_b_finetune.json` and its markdown rendering; this document
reads them and says what they mean. Where the two disagree, the JSON is right.*

**Run:** Bio_ClinicalBERT (`d5892b39a4adaed74b92212a44081509db72f87b`), mean
pooling, `max_seq_len` 256, fp32, 3 epochs, batch 32, lr 2e-5, strict
determinism, RTX 5070. Five folds over fragment clusters, 10,000/2,000/2,000
examples per fold. Ruleset hash `325b3306…`.

---

## Answer

**Both, in that order, and the library half is not where the plan expected it.**

Unfreezing the encoder was worth a lot: decisive accuracy went from 71.6% to
83.5% and macro-F1 from 68.0% to 82.1%. So the frozen pooled representation was
a genuine constraint, and Arm A alone would have understated what this data can
support by about twelve points.

But what Arm B has left is not spread thinly, which is what "the method is still
too weak" would look like. It is concentrated: **1,157 errors on 96 of 394
decisive fragments, and half of all of them on 17 fragments.** An even spread
across the fragments that erred at all would have put half the errors on 48.
Arm B is right about most of the library and wrong about a short, nameable list.

And that list is mostly **`fever_true` and `fever_false`** — 824 of 1,157 errors,
71% — not the ambiguous confounders the four hard libraries were written for. The
prediction on file was the reverse.

**The next month is library work on the clear classes.** Details in §5.

---

## 1. Headline

Pooled over five folds, so every fragment cluster is a test cluster exactly once
and the aggregate test set is the whole library. Intervals are the cluster
bootstrap. Read the **decisive** slice: `overall` includes structural nulls,
which share one resampling unit by design and swing the interval on nothing.

| model | decisive acc [95% CI] | decisive macro-F1 | overall acc | per-fold sd |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `majority_class` | 43.6% [37.9, 49.7] | 20.2% | 60.4% | 0.2% |
| `length_only` | 44.1% [38.7, 50.1] | 22.1% | 60.7% | 0.3% |
| `tfidf_logreg` | 70.0% [65.2, 75.0] | 66.5% | 78.7% | 3.3% |
| `arm_a_probe` | 71.6% [68.2, 75.0] | 68.0% | 79.7% | 2.7% |
| **`arm_b_finetune`** | **83.5% [79.1, 88.0]** | **82.1%** | **88.4%** | **4.4%** |

Decisive n = 7,022 examples, **eff n = 349 clusters**. The second number is the
sample size; see §6.

**Per class, Arm B, after the decision rule:**

| class | support | predicted | precision | recall | F1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `false` | 2,479 | 2,069 | 93.6% | 78.1% | 85.2% |
| `true` | 1,481 | 1,765 | 67.9% | 81.0% | 73.9% |
| `null` | 6,040 | 6,166 | 92.5% | 94.4% | 93.4% |

`null → true`: 253 of 6,040 truly-null examples (4.19%), down from 5.55% at raw
argmax. That is the cell that invents a symptom into a patient's pre-filled form.
The rule bought 1.4 points of it and the selected margin was 0.9 — near the top
of the grid — on four folds of five. The rule wanted to be much more
conservative than argmax, and it still lands above TF-IDF's 1.27%. Worth knowing
before anyone treats these weights as deployable.

## 2. Negative controls: both pass

**Shuffled training labels, scored on the unpermuted test split.** Arm B's
control drove training loss to 1e-4 — 110M parameters memorising a permutation,
exactly as expected — *and* scored 60.1–60.8% on test, which is the majority
class to within noise, macro-F1 25%. Both halves, which is what a pass requires:
near-zero training loss alone is not a failure and chance test performance alone
is not a pass. The TF-IDF and length controls sit at 43.6% decisive, identical to
majority.

**Disjointness** was asserted at load on every run: no fragment and no cluster
appears in two splits of a fold, and no cluster is a test cluster in two folds.

Nothing here is the pipeline scoring itself.

## 3. The paired comparison, and a trap in it

McNemar on raw argmax, over the examples the two models disagree about.

| pair | slice | n | a only | b only | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `arm_a_probe` vs `arm_b_finetune` | `null_ambiguous` | 3,062 | 217 | 336 | 4.7e-07 |
| `tfidf_logreg` vs `arm_b_finetune` | `null_ambiguous` | 3,062 | 311 | 82 | 1.7e-32 |
| `tfidf_logreg` vs `arm_b_finetune` | overall | 10,000 | 518 | 1,380 | 4.1e-90 |
| `tfidf_logreg` vs `arm_a_probe` | overall | 10,000 | 894 | 971 | 0.078 |

Read the second row carefully, because taken alone it says bag-of-words beats the
fine-tuned transformer on the slice the whole ticket was pointed at, and on
accuracy it does: 94.9% against 89.1%.

**It is an artefact of null bias, and the report warns about it twice.** Every
example in `null_ambiguous` is truly `null`, so answering `null` unconditionally
scores 100% — `majority_class` does exactly that. TF-IDF predicts `null` 7,533
times against a support of 6,040 and buys its score there with `true` recall of
47.3% and `false` recall of 52.8%. Arm B predicts `null` 6,166 times with recalls
of 81.0% and 78.1%. The two models are not making the same trade, so their
accuracies on an all-null slice are not comparable.

The comparison that survives that is the decisive slice, where both classes are
present: Arm B is **+13.5 points of accuracy and +15.6 of macro-F1** over TF-IDF,
and McNemar overall is 1,380 to 518, p = 4.1e-90. That is the transformer earning
its keep, and it is not close.

Note also row four: **Arm A does not beat TF-IDF overall** (p = 0.078). Had we
built only the frozen probe, the honest conclusion would have been "a transformer
is not measurably better than bag-of-words here", and it would have been wrong.
That is the whole argument for Arm B existing, and it is now a result rather than
an argument.

## 4. Where the errors are

**Arm B, by library:**

| library | eff n | accuracy | errors |
|---|---|---|---|
| `fever_false` | 60 | 78.1% | 542 |
| `fever_true` | 96 | 81.0% | 282 |
| `fever_null_hedged` | 32 | 75.6% | 136 |
| `fever_null_thirdparty` | 35 | 90.5% | 60 |
| `fever_null_metaphor` | 47 | 92.0% | 54 |
| `fever_null_historical` | 36 | 92.1% | 47 |
| `fever_null_attribution` | 43 | 94.0% | 36 |

**Sub-class recall, pooled** (Arm A → Arm B): hedged 67.6% → **75.6%**,
historical 80.7% → **92.1%**, metaphor 89.8% → **92.0%**, third_party 89.4% →
**90.5%**, attribution 88.8% → **94.0%**. Unfreezing helped on every one.

The four confounder libraries the plan was most worried about are now the *strong*
part of the model. What it gets wrong is clear positives and clear negatives.

**Reading the worst fragments** — the reason the per-fragment table exists — the
residual is not random. Three families:

**Negations that contain a positive.** Five `fever_false` fragments are wrong on
every example they appear in:

> "My mum was really poorly with a fever last week and I was caring for her but I
> never got one myself" → `null`
>
> "Back when I caught that bug going round the office I felt like I was on fire,
> but I've not had anything like that with this" → `true`
>
> "I know what it's like when I get a temperature because I go all clammy and
> weird, and that definitely hasn't happened" → `null`

Every one is a contrastive construction: a fever asserted of someone else or of
some other time, then negated for this patient now. The model reads the first
clause. These are the hardest sentences in the library and there are almost none
of them — `fever_false` has 60 clusters total and no cluster markers.

**Positives where the claim is idiom rather than vocabulary.**

> "Came down with something Tuesday night, been like a hot water bottle ever
> since." → `null`
>
> "Thermometer said I was cooking, way above normal." → `null`

No fever word appears. The model has learned a vocabulary and these sentences
sit outside it.

**Positives buried in urgency.**

> "I keep getting these hot flushes and my partner says I'm on fire, I can't
> function properly and I've got three important meetings I can't miss." → `null`

Which is the urgency leak `arch_training.md` §9 describes, firing in the opposite
direction to the one predicted: the justification clause did not make positives
easier to spot, it drowned them.

**`fever_null_hedged` is the one confounder library still failing** at 75.6%
[60.3, 89.0] over 32 clusters — the widest interval in the table and the smallest
library. Its failures split both ways ("I'm not entirely certain whether or not
I've got a high temperature" → `true` 10 times, `false` 5), which is what
genuine uncertainty should look like when a model has too few examples of it.

## 5. What the next month should contain

**Library work on the clear classes, not the confounders.** Specifically:

1. **Contrastive negatives.** Write 30–40 `fever_false` fragments of the form
   "someone else / some other time had a fever, I do not now". This is the single
   largest error family and the library has almost no coverage of it. Cluster-tag
   them; they will produce near-duplicates by construction.
2. **Non-vocabulary positives.** Fever asserted through idiom, thermometer
   readings, or physical description with no fever word. Currently the model has
   memorised a lexicon.
3. **Grow `fever_null_hedged`.** 32 clusters is the smallest confounder library
   and the worst-performing, and its interval is too wide to act on.
4. Leave metaphor, historical, third-party and attribution alone. They are at
   90–94% and are not where the loss is.

**Not model work.** Nothing in these numbers suggests a bigger model or a
different recipe would help: the errors are concentrated on fragments whose
underlying idea is barely represented, and no amount of parameter count invents
training data.

**The ticket after that, unchanged and still the important one:** 60–100
hand-written realistic full submissions, deliberately unlike the recombinations,
labelled by hand, held out and never touched by a training decision. Everything
above is scored on recombinations of the same few hundred fragments the model
trained on. Held-out clusters remove memorisation and nothing else — the test
examples are still short, still one supervised claim plus filler, still in the
same register. **83.5% here is not a claim about real patient text.**

## 6. Scoring the recorded prediction

The plan recorded what it expected before any run, so it could be scored rather
than rationalised. It was substantially wrong, and usefully so.

| Prediction | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Overall test accuracy 90%+ | **Missed.** 88.4% overall, 83.5% decisive. |
| Near-perfect on `null_structural` | **Held.** 99.9%. |
| Near-perfect on clear positives and negatives | **Wrong, and it is the important miss.** `true` 81.0%, `false` 78.1%. The plan assumed the easy classes were solved and only ambiguity was hard; 71% of Arm B's errors are on them. |
| Bag-of-words within a few points overall | **Wrong.** Arm B is +9.7 overall and +13.5 decisive. |
| The transformer's only real advantage is on `null_ambiguous` | **Wrong, and inverted.** Its advantage is largest on the clear classes (`true` recall 47.3% → 81.0%). On `null_ambiguous` accuracy it loses to TF-IDF, for the null-bias reason in §3. |
| Weakest on metaphor and hay fever | **Wrong.** Metaphor is 92.0%, one of the better sub-classes. The weakest is `hedged` at 75.6%. |
| Arm A does badly on the hard `null` sub-classes | **Held.** hedged 67.6%, historical 80.7%. |
| Arm B less stable across folds than Arm A | **Held.** sd 4.4% against 2.7%. |
| Arm B's control passes by two things at once | **Held.** Training loss 1e-4 *and* chance on test. |

The pattern in the misses is one mistake: the plan assumed the confounder
libraries were the hard part because they were the part we had thought hardest
about. Writing four libraries of deliberate confounders made the model good at
confounders. The clear classes got no such attention and are now the bottleneck.

## 7. Limitations

*Reproduced in full from `arch_training.md` §9 and §10 rather than
cross-referenced, because this document will be read on its own.*

**Effective n, not n, bounds every number above.** A slice's example count says
how much recombination happened. Its effective n — distinct hand-written fragment
clusters — says how many independent ideas were tested, and that is what the
bootstrap resamples. 7,022 decisive examples is **349 ideas** seen many times.

**Folds bought about a factor of three on the error bar, not twelve.** Pooling
five folds raises each hard sub-class from 2–6 test clusters to its whole library,
32–47. Uncertainty on a proportion falls as 1/√n, so roughly ±30 points becomes
roughly **±8**. That is the difference between a number that can carry a
conclusion and one that cannot; it is not a twelve-fold gain in precision. Every
sub-class figure in §4 should be read with ±8 attached.

**The pooled result is slightly flattered.** Fold *i*'s validation clusters are
fold *i+1*'s test clusters, so each fold's decision margin was selected on a
sibling fold's test data. Within a fold there is no leakage — each trains its own
model and never sees its own test bucket. Nested cross-validation would remove
the optimism and was judged not worth it for one scalar per fold.

**The across-fold sd is a stability check, not a CI.** Four degrees of freedom.
Arm B's 4.4% is real instability — folds 0 and 2 scored 93.7% and 92.7%, folds 1,
3 and 4 scored 84.9%, 85.2% and 85.5% — but do not read that spread as an
interval.

**McNemar's pairing unit is the example, not the cluster.** It answers "did these
two models behave differently on this data", which is narrower than "would they
differ on new fragments". Where a slice is recombinations of a few clusters it
overstates significance in exactly the way the cluster bootstrap avoids.

**Fold mode trains on 60% of clusters; the legacy split uses 70%.** These numbers
are not comparable to any single-split figure in `arch_training.md` §10.

**The validation score is a smoke test, not evidence.** The training plan asks for
~200 fragments per signal. We have 96 for `true` and 60 for `false`.

**Length may still leak.** `fever_true` fragments run 3 to 98 words; the
`fever_null` libraries sit in a 9–40 band. Medians are close (16 against 15–19),
so it is a tail problem — but the `length_only` baseline measures how much is
there to notice, and at 44.1% decisive against majority's 43.6% the answer is
**almost nothing**. That is the first direct measurement of this leak and it is
reassuring.

**Urgency language leaks.** ~17% of `fever_true` fragments bundle the claim with
a justification, against 8% of `fever_false` and almost none of `fever_null`. §4
shows it firing.

**The examples are short.** Median 36 tokens against `max_seq_len` 256. Sequence
length is not the constraint and raising it fixes nothing. Real submissions are
longer and messier, and that gap is unmeasured.

**Only `fever_present` is covered**, and a single head cannot satisfy
`EncoderOutput.validate_against` — `data/uti1.json` declares seven
`send_to_encoder` signals. Swapping in a real encoder stays blocked until all
seven exist or the contract permits partial output.

**The base model revision was not pinned on the command line.** The resolved SHA
`d5892b39a4adaed74b92212a44081509db72f87b` is recorded in the sidecar, so this run
is reproducible after the fact; pass `--revision` explicitly next time.
