# Character-level noise: the 2×2 and the rate sweep

*Write-up of the thirteen-cell noise sweep run on 2026-08-31 (12.6, task 5).
Every number here comes from the thirteen `fever_present.arm_b_finetune.md` and
four `fever_present.baselines.md` files under `reports/encoder_training/noise/`.
**Those files are the authority** — where this document disagrees with one of
them, the report is right and this is stale. Unlike the 2026-08-19 run there are
no companion JSON artefacts for these cells; the rendered reports are the record.*

*Reads on from `documentation/encoder_plans/random_error_generation_implementation.md`,
which is the plan this run executes, and from the two commits that carry the
run's own notes: `6b0f201` (the DD9 gate) and `c95c113` (the thirteen cells and
the diagonal reading).*

**Run:** `roberta-base`, single-head `fever_present`, every layer unfrozen, 3
epochs, five folds over fragment clusters, `GENERATOR_VERSION` 3, `--train-seed
1234`, `--determinism strict`. One clean fold tree and four noised trees built
from it by `scripts/synthetic_data/noise.py` at `--seed 42`, `--clean-share
0.25`: **r03**, **r06** and **r12** at `--rate` 0.03, 0.06 and 0.12 under
`--freeze-signal-vocabulary short`, plus **freezeall** at 0.06 under
`--freeze-signal-vocabulary all`. Every tree rests on the same fragments, the
same clusters and the same fold assignment, so **every cell below is n 7022 and
eff n 418** — the same held-out clusters throughout, differing only in surface
form. Off-diagonal cells were run lean, with the test split swapped in via
`--test-dir`. The whole sweep ran on transformers 5.14.1.

The two `r03-on-r06` and `r03-on-r12` cells (section 6) were run separately,
after the original thirteen, on the same stack and the same flags, with
`--base-model roberta-base` and `--revision` pinned to
`e2da8e2f811d1448a5b465c236feacd80ffbac7b` — the SHA the original thirteen
resolved to without pinning. **`--base-model` is not optional on this CLI**: it
defaults to `emilyalsentzer/Bio_ClinicalBERT`, and a cell that inherits the
default is not comparable with anything in the matrix below.

---

## Answer

**Damage costs the current model a lot, training on damage recovers essentially
all of it, and it costs nothing on clean text.**

A clean-trained model loses **8.5 points** of decisive accuracy on text damaged
at 12% per word — 93.3% to 84.8% — and 9.5 points of macro-F1. A model trained
at the same rate scores **93.3%** on that same damaged text: the entire loss is
recovered. On clean text the noise-trained models score 93.5 / 93.8 / 94.3
against the clean-trained 93.3, so the recovery is free.

Nothing degrades across the rates tested. The three matched-condition cells span
0.6 points (93.4 / 92.8 / 93.3) against a clean-on-clean 93.3. The
subword-shattering ceiling the plan warned about does not appear anywhere
between 1% and 12% per-word damage.

The failure the noise causes has a direction, and it is the quiet one: a damaged
message does not become a denial, it becomes "not mentioned". `true` recall
falls 90.5% → 65.3% while the `null → true` rate *falls* from 2.42% to 1.14%.

**The one question this run does not answer is whether one rate covers another**
— every noise-trained model was only ever scored at its own rate. Section 6 is
open pending two evaluations.

## 1. The gate, before any score

DD9 requires the noise pass to prove that edit density has not become a proxy
for the label. Commit `6b0f201` ran that gate on the baselines before any GPU
time, and it returned a qualified pass that changes what DD9 is allowed to
claim:

* `length_only` is **flat to the decimal across all four trees** — 43.9%
  decisive, 21.4% `null_ambiguous`, 60.6% overall, 3939/3938/3939/3939 errors —
  at up to 12.8 edits per hundred words. On the channel a length baseline can
  see, damage carries no label information.
* `tfidf_logreg` degrades monotonically with the rate (73.5 → 73.4 → 73.1 →
  72.3 decisive), which confirms the runs are reading the damaged text and that
  the damage destroys lexical signal rather than manufacturing label signal.
* **But the by-label edit rate is not equal**, and the inequality is structural
  rather than sampling noise: `null` is the highest of the three classes in 20
  of 20 fold files. The direction is the frozen lexicon working as designed —
  `null` examples are filler carrying no signal vocabulary, so nothing is
  refused and they take the full damage. `true` against `false` shows no
  consistent ordering, so the two classes that must be separated on evidence
  have no density difference between them.

**Consequence for the docs: DD9's "equality holds by construction" is too
strong** and should be corrected in 12.6. The lexicon guarantees that no
rejection is label-aware; it does not guarantee equal aggregate rates, because
word composition differs by class. What the gate establishes is the weaker and
sufficient claim: the density gap exists, it runs `null`-high, and it does not
separate `true` from `false`.

The sidecars carrying the per-tree by-label tables live under
`data/synthetic/generated/`, which is gitignored, so `6b0f201`'s commit message
is the record of them.

## 2. The predictions, scored

Two predictions were recorded in `6b0f201` before the 2×2 was read, both chosen
because they are what a model *exploiting* the density gap would violate. The
off-diagonal cells add a third test that the diagonals could not perform.

| prediction | result |
|---|---|
| **1.** Train-noisy/test-noisy should not beat train-clean/test-clean | **HELD.** 93.4 / 92.8 / 93.3 against 93.3. The four diagonals span 0.6 points with near-identical intervals. |
| **2.** Noisy-trained models should not over-predict `null` on the noisy test split | **HELD.** Predicted `null` 6138 / 6007 / 6122 against a support of 6040 — non-monotone, all within 1.6%, and r06 under-predicts. |
| **3.** *(new, from the off-diagonals)* Noisy-trained models should not under-predict `null` on the **clean** test split | **HELD.** 6142 / 6007 / 6097 against 6040. |

Prediction 3 is the sharper of the three and only became available with the
off-diagonal cells. A model that had learned "heavily damaged ⇒ `null`" would
carry that rule to a test set where nothing is damaged and nothing looks like
the cue, and would then answer `null` too rarely. It does not: the noise-trained
models scatter around the support on clean text with no trend, and the
clean-trained control sits at 6057 in the middle of them. The shortcut the gate
was built to detect is not present in the trained models.

Decisive macro-F1 tells the identical story and is not tabulated twice: the

92.1 / 92.7 / 90.8.

Two structural points about this table before any of it is read as a result.

**The six blank cells are not missing data, they are the design.** Training
depends only on the training tree, so the sweep is five trained models evaluated
against two or three test trees each — twenty training runs and forty
evaluations, not one run per cell. The plan's correction to 12.6's arithmetic
(change 3) is what bought the rate sweep rather than paying twice for the same
models.

**Noise creates no new clusters.** Effective n is identical in every cell
because the noised trees carry the same example ids, fragment ids and cluster
assignment as the clean one — that identity is asserted by `swap_test_split`
before any `--test-dir` evaluation is allowed to produce a number. This caps
what a win can mean: a gain here is robustness to damaged surface form and never
better coverage of the clinical space.

## 4. The four comparisons

The plan requires this table to be read as four comparisons rather than one
number, in a stated order.

### 4.1 What the clean-trained model is losing to damage

This is the comparison the plan says to run first, because it carries a stop
condition: *if the clean-trained model barely drops on noisy text, stop, there
is nothing here to buy.*

| test tree | decisive acc | vs clean | macro-F1 | vs clean |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| clean | 93.3% | — | 92.7% | — |
| r03 | 89.0% | **−4.3** | 88.0% | −4.7 |
| r06 | 86.0% | **−7.3** | 84.6% | −8.1 |
| r12 | 84.8% | **−8.5** | 83.2% | −9.5 |

**The stop condition does not fire.** The drop is monotone in the rate, and at
all three rates the damaged cell's interval fails to reach the clean cell's point
estimate of 93.3 (upper bounds 91.4, 88.7, 87.5). There is something here to buy.

### 4.2 Does training on damaged text buy robustness to damaged text?

This is the ticket's claim. Each row compares two models on the *same* test
tree, so the only difference is what they were trained on.

| test tree | clean-trained | rate-matched | gain | recovery |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| r03 | 89.0 [86.5, 91.4] | **93.4** [91.4, 95.3] | +4.4 | 102% |
| r06 | 86.0 [83.1, 88.7] | **92.8** [90.7, 94.8] | +6.8 | 93% |
| r12 | 84.8 [81.8, 87.5] | **93.3** [91.4, 95.1] | +8.5 | 100% |

"Recovery" is the gain as a share of the loss in 4.1 — how much of what damage
took away is given back.

**It buys robustness, and it buys essentially all of it.** The intervals are
cleanly separated at r06 and r12; at r03 they meet exactly at 91.4 and the gain
is the smallest of the three, so r03 is the weakest-supported of the three rows
and the reading rests on r06 and r12. These are unpaired comparisons of two
separately trained models, which is the weaker form; because the two models are
scored on the identical 7022 examples and 418 clusters, a paired test would be
sharper still, and the tooling cannot currently perform one across two report
runs (section 8).

### 4.3 What does it cost on text that is fine?

| trained on | decisive acc on clean | vs clean-trained |
|---|---|---|
| clean | 93.3 [91.2, 95.3] | — |
| r03 | 93.5 [91.3, 95.4] | +0.2 |
| r06 | 93.8 [91.7, 95.7] | +0.5 |
| r12 | 94.3 [92.3, 96.1] | +1.0 |

**No detectable cost.** All four intervals are nested. The trend is faintly
positive and 1.0 point on eff n 418 is not a finding; the honest reading is that
noise training is free on clean text, not that it helps.

### 4.4 Across rates

The plan is explicit that "a little noise helps and more does not" and "noise
helps" are different findings that one rate cannot distinguish.

| rate | matched-condition acc | on clean |
|---|---|---|
| clean | 93.3 | 93.3 |
| r03 | 93.4 | 93.5 |
| r06 | 92.8 | 93.8 |
| r12 | 93.3 | 94.3 |

**Flat.** The finding is "noise helps", not "a little noise helps". The plan's
first predicted honest-null outcome — that a subword tokenizer shatters a
misspelt word into pieces carrying little of the original meaning, so above some
rate this becomes training on noise rather than on harder text — **does not
occur anywhere in the range tested**. r12 is as good as r03 on its own test set
and the best of the four on clean text. Locating that ceiling would need rates
well above 12% per word, which is already past what a phone keyboard plausibly
produces.

One contrast worth recording beside this. On the diagonals, Arm A — the frozen
probe — falls monotonically with the damage rate (77.1 → 75.3 → 74.7 → 74.3)
while Arm B stays flat, and `tfidf_logreg` falls too (73.5 → 72.3). A frozen
encoder and a bag of words cannot adapt to damaged tokens; a fine-tune absorbs
them at no cost to accuracy. That is the mechanism behind 4.2 stated on the
diagonals alone.

## 5. The direction of the failure

The most useful thing in the off-diagonal cells is not the size of the loss in
4.1 but its shape. A clean-trained model reading damaged text does not get
confused about the answer — it stops answering.

Recall after the decision rule, clean-trained model, by test tree:

| test tree | `true` recall | `false` recall | `null → true` | predicted `null` (support 6040) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| clean | 90.5% | 94.4% | 2.42% | 6057 |
| r03 | 75.8% | 88.5% | 1.49% | 6482 |
| r06 | 68.1% | 83.2% | 1.06% | 6758 |
| r12 | **65.3%** | **81.4%** | **1.14%** | **6842** |

Every point of the loss in 4.1 is decisive recall draining into `null`. The
`null → true` rate — the cell that invents a symptom into a patient's pre-filled
form, and the one the decision rule treats as a constraint rather than something
to trade against F1 — *improves* under damage, because the model is answering
"not mentioned" more often about everything. Per library, `fever_true` accuracy
falls 90.5% → 65.3% and `fever_false` 94.4% → 81.4% between the clean and r12
test trees, while all five `null` sub-class libraries rise towards 100%.

A rate-matched model restores both: r12-on-r12 reads `fever_true` at 85.7% and
`fever_false` at 96.5%.

Two consequences.

**Clinically this is the safer of the two failure directions and it is still a
real loss.** A typo'd "I've had a temperature since Tuesday" does not become a
denial; it becomes silence, and the pre-filled form simply does not carry the
fever. At r12 the model is dropping a third of the true fevers it would
otherwise find.

**The `null_ambiguous` slice is uninformative in the off-diagonal cells and must
not be quoted from them.** Clean-trained accuracy on that slice *rises* under
damage — 93.8% → 96.8% → 96.9% — for exactly the wrong reason: `majority_class`
scores 100% on it, so a model drifting towards `null` is flattered. It is the
headline slice for the ticket 6 question and it is an artefact here.

**A note on `null → true` between training trees.** On the clean test split the
rate runs 2.42% (clean-trained), 1.57% (r03), 2.81% (r06), 1.72% (r12), 2.68%
(freezeall). There is no trend, and the spread across five models scored on
identical data is larger than any difference attributable to noise training.
Read that spread as the run-to-run variation of this quantity, and do not read
r06-on-r06's 3.05% as a cost of noise training — that model reads 2.81% on clean
text too.

## 6. Does the rate transfer?

**Yes, and nearly completely.** This section was written as a hole when the
original thirteen cells were read: every noise-trained model had been scored only
against its own rate, so the run could not distinguish "training on damage buys
general robustness" from "you have to match the rate". Two evaluations close it.
The r03-trained models — the *lightest* inoculation in the sweep — scored against
the r06 and r12 test trees:

| test tree | clean-trained | **r03-trained** | rate-matched | r03 vs matched | loss recovered |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| r06 (2× the training rate) | 86.0 | **93.1** [91.1, 95.1] | 92.8 | +0.3 | **97%** |
| r12 (4× the training rate) | 84.8 | **92.2** [90.1, 94.2] | 93.3 | −1.1 | **87%** |

"Loss recovered" is against the 93.3 clean-on-clean ceiling, as in 4.2.

**The rate does not have to be guessed right.** At 2× the training rate the
transfer cell is indistinguishable from the rate-matched model. At 4× it is 1.1
points below it — a hint of decay with distance, on overlapping intervals
(92.2 [90.1, 94.2] against 93.3 [91.4, 95.1]), so the decay is a direction rather
than a measurement. What is not in doubt is the size of what transfers: a model
that has only ever seen 3% damage recovers 87% of what 12% damage takes from an
uninoculated model.

This is the result that makes the ticket usable. The real damage rate in patient
submissions is unmeasured and probably unmeasurable — it is whatever people type
into a `<textarea>` on a phone — so a fix that only worked at a matched rate would
have been of little practical value. It does not need matching.

## 7. The `--freeze-signal-vocabulary` variant

Change 1 in the plan — splitting the protected list into frozen tokens and
damageable signal vocabulary, so that "temperature" can become "temprature"
while "hot" and "not" cannot — is the one design decision the plan flags as
arguable, and it shipped with the conservative alternative one flag away
precisely so it could be measured. The freezeall tree is 12.6's literal rule at
the middle rate.

| | short (r06) | all (freezeall) |
|---|---|---|
| damage done to a clean-trained model | 86.2 → 86.0, i.e. **the same** | 86.2 |
| rate-matched accuracy | **92.8** [90.7, 94.8] | 91.7 [89.5, 93.6] |
| shortfall against the 93.3 ceiling | **0.5** | 1.6 |
| macro-F1, rate-matched | **92.1** | 90.8 |
| on clean text | **93.8** | 93.0 |
| `null → true`, rate-matched | **3.05%** | 3.31% |

**Freezing the whole signal vocabulary buys nothing measurable, and may cost a
little.** The first row is the important one: the two test trees damage a
clean-trained model by an identical amount, so `all` does not make the test text
easier — the damage is equally destructive, it just lands somewhere else. What
differs is recovery, and `short` recovers more completely on both metrics and on
both test trees.

The intervals overlap, so this is **"no reason to prefer `all`"** rather than
"`short` wins". That is enough to settle the question the plan asked: the
loosening in change 1 is safe, `short` stays the default, and the conservative
rule is not quietly better. The plan's own fallback advice — "if in doubt, ship
`all`" — is discharged by measurement rather than left standing.

## 8. What this run does not establish

* **One signal.** Everything here is `fever_present`. The frozen lexicon covers
  all seven signals via `lint.SIGNAL_LEXICONS`, but nothing has been trained on
  the other six under noise, and fever's libraries are the two untagged ones
  (`fever_true`, `fever_false` carry no cluster markers at all), so its eff n is
  an upper bound and its intervals are narrower than the truth.
* **The comparisons in 4.2 are unpaired.** The two models being compared live in
  two separate report runs, and nothing in `scripts/encoder_training/` performs
  a McNemar test across runs. Within a report the paired machinery exists and is
  used; across the 2×2 it does not. The test examples are id-identical by
  construction, so this is a tooling gap rather than a design one, and closing
  it would tighten every comparison in section 4.
* **The real-text holdout is not readable here and must not be reported as a
  result.** The `null → true` rates of 23.7% (clean), 11.0% (r03), 25.3% (r06),
  14.3% (r12) look like a finding and are not: the within-cell fold spread is
  larger than every between-cell difference (the clean cell alone runs 5 to 26
  of 49; r06 runs 2 to 25), there is no monotone pattern, decisive n is 18, and
  the worst-case half-width is ±23.1 points. The set cannot rank two models, as
  its own preamble says. It is also unchanged by `--test-dir` — the holdout is
  scored on real text, so it varies only with the training tree, and the fact
  that clean-on-clean and clean-on-r12 report identical holdout figures is a
  correctness check on the plumbing rather than a result.
* **Nothing here is real damage.** This pass generates mechanical single-character
  errors. The free-text box in `frontend/src/screens/EditScreen.tsx` is a plain
  `<textarea>` with browser spellcheck on, typed on a phone with autocorrect on
  top, so a large share of the nonword typos measured here would never reach us.
  The errors that survive that filter are disproportionately **real-word** errors
  — autocorrect substitutions, homophones, dropped words — which are a different
  generator and, on this evidence, the more valuable one to build. This run
  measures the cheap half of the problem and should be quoted as such.
* **Fold-mode optimism applies to every margin here**, as in every run in this
  project: each fold's decision margin was selected on a sibling fold's test
  clusters. It applies equally to every cell, so it does not explain any gap in
  section 4, but no absolute figure here is a deployment estimate.
* **The rate is not a measurement of anything.** 3%, 6% and 12% per word were
  chosen to span a plausible range, not fitted to observed patient text, and no
  measurement of the real damage rate in submissions exists. Section 6 is what
  makes that tolerable rather than fatal — the transfer result means the range
  only has to be roughly right — but nothing here establishes that 12% is an
  upper bound on real damage, and a submission far messier than r12 is outside
  everything measured.

## 9. What follows

1. 1. **Adopt a noised training tree, and train at the top of the plausible damage
   range rather than the middle.** Sections 4.3 and 6 together make this close to
   free: training heavy costs nothing on clean text and covers more of the range
   than training light. Nothing in the sweep argues for the middle.
2. **Ship `short` as the default and 12.6's literal rule as the documented
   alternative.** Section 7 settles this; no further cells are needed.
3. **Do not adopt a noised training tree for the other six signals on this
   evidence yet.** The result is free on clean text and clearly positive on
   damaged text for one signal whose libraries are the least well cluster-tagged
   in the project. Extending it is cheap; what it needs is one signal's worth of
   confirmation that the effect is not a property of fever's libraries.
4. **Build the cross-run paired comparison.** The 2×2's central claim currently
   rests on non-overlapping unpaired intervals when the examples are
   id-identical and a paired test is available in principle. This is the single
   change that would most improve any future surface-form experiment.
5. **Consider the real-word error generator.** Section 8 argues it is where the
   remaining value is: the errors that survive spellcheck and autocorrect are
   the ones that will actually reach the encoder, and this pass generates almost
   none of them.
6. **Record library versions in the artefacts.** The whole sweep's comparability
   rests on a sentence in a commit message saying it ran on transformers 5.14.1.
   Nothing in the sidecars records it, and section 6 has already had to inherit
   that constraint by hand.
